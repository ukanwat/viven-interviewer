How the interviewer works, and why it's built this way

The goal: talk with someone for twenty minutes, by voice, and come away with notes good enough for a digital twin to answer the way they would. It has to work for anyone, not just people who explain themselves well. Five things shaped the design. Each one started as a mistake.

1

Let the voice model lead

Before

The note-taker wrote every question and the voice read it out. Replies took 2–8 seconds, and sometimes two questions came out at once.

Now

The voice model answers on its own, because it can hear when someone has finished. The note-taker sends a hint for the next question.

2

Leave behaviour to the model, not code

Before

Code rules stopped repeats, capped topics and trimmed long questions. Each one broke a normal conversation somewhere.

Now

The prompt describes what a good interviewer does, and the model decides. Code only moves and saves data.

3

Keep notes in their own words

Before

It saved labelled facts: a value, a rule, a story. Much of it was about the call ("didn't like being interrupted"), not the person.

Now

A small folder of short notes (work, family, views) in their words, tidied against the transcript after every call.

4

Ask about what makes them different

Before

In my own test call it asked five questions about how I prioritise work. Anyone would have said the same.

Now

It starts with what the person is known for, sketches the rest of their life, and digs only where an answer is unusual.

5

Make the screen calm

Before

The first screen looked like a debugging tool, and people performed for it.

Now

Type your name and talk. Notes appear beside the conversation as they're written. Come back later and it picks up where you left off.

How I check a change

Ten simulated people with hidden facts are interviewed by the real system. Another model answers questions about each person from the notes alone, and a model from a different company grades the answers.
